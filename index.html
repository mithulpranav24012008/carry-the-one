1,234 × 56, cover off

56 is 100 minus 44, so the machine turns back four times at the units, back four at the tens and forward once at the hundreds. Nine turns instead of eleven. This is short-cut multiplication, and operators did it by hand. The counter still ends on 56 and the accumulator on 69,104.

Three moves

All arithmetic on this machine comes down to these. Move the sliders and watch the numbers change.

Three centuries of turning

Carry is an invented machine, drawn in the line of these four. By the 1970s the pocket electronic calculator had ended the line.

Forty-one parts to read

Ten wheels you set, ten that count, twenty that answer, and one bell.

Ten to set

The setting wheels show what you keyed. Up to ten digits.

Ten to count

The counter records the turns. After a division, it holds the answer.

Twenty to answer

The accumulator takes every turn, on a carriage that shifts by columns.

One bell

It rings when a subtraction goes through zero. Division listens for it.

Questions

What does the Scientific Calculator button do?

The machine's own parts rebuild themselves into an electronic scientific calculator, and press it again to turn back. The cover's planks become the body, the crank becomes the stand, and the keys re-form into a new keypad, with the spare keys sliding out from inside the ones you already know. It works as a real calculator with trig in degrees, radians or grads, logs, roots, powers, factorials, brackets, memory and Ans. From the keyboard: s, c and t for trig, l for log, n for ln, r for root, p for π, ^ for powers and Enter for equals. Lift its cover and it comes apart down to the logic board: type a sum and you can watch the signal run along each key's row and column, through the chip and out to the display.

Why does 1 ÷ 3 × 3 give 0.999999999?

Because that is what the wheels say. A division stops when the counter runs out of columns, and the machine does not round up. The figure stays visibly short of 1, as it would have on a real desk in 1955.

Does it really calculate with wheels?

Every digit is added column by column, with each carry passed to the next wheel, and the 3D wheels are positioned from those same digits. There is no floating point, so 0.1 + 0.2 comes to exactly 0.3.

What happens below zero?

The bell rings. A true machine would then show a long run of nines, the tens complement. Carry adds one convenience: its wheels roll over to the plain figure and a red flag rises in the window beside the counter.

Can it divide by zero?

Try it. Division subtracts until the bell rings, and subtracting zero never rings it. A real machine had to be stopped by hand. This one turns nine times and jams until you press AC.

Which keys does it take?

Digits, the point and + − * / from your keyboard, Enter for equals, Delete for C and Escape for AC. The arrow keys shift the carriage. N changes the sign. The crank is yours: set a number and drag the handle round once to add it by hand. To see it from any side, drag the machine, or use the Front, Back, Left, Right, Top and Under buttons. Lift the cover and every part floats free: click one to see it alone, and use the arrow keys to move between parts and Escape to go back. Pinch, or hold Control and scroll, to zoom.

Is this a copy of a real calculator?

No. Its stepped drums, shifting carriage, counter and bell all come from real machines, but this model, the name and the maker's plate are invented. The red sign flag is the one thing no machine of the period had in this form.
